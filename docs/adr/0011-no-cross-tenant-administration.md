# No cross-tenant administration

Volt has no global administrator role, and will not gain one without replacing this record. Every authorisation decision in the system is organisation-scoped: `getOrganisationAccess` asks whether a user holds a sufficient role in *this* organisation, and there is no path that answers a broader question. The consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the property being protected — a stolen session reaches exactly the organisations that user belongs to, and no others. There is no account in the system whose compromise exposes every tenant's energy and settlement data.

The cost is accepted deliberately. Nobody can answer "which organisations does this user belong to" without being a member of them, support cannot look up an account, and there is no way to suspend a user across the platform. Those are real operational gaps. They are preferable to a role that, once it exists, must be defended forever and re-checked on every endpoint added afterwards; the moment such a role is introduced, every future route has to reason about it, and the failure mode of getting that wrong is cross-tenant disclosure rather than a bug. Should the gaps become intolerable, the replacement should be the narrowest thing that closes them — a read-only support view over the user directory that cannot read ledger, simulation, or audit data, granted out of band, and auditing every access — not an administrator that can enter organisations.

Account self-management therefore belongs to the account holder alone. Better Auth already serves most of it through the proxied `/api/auth/*` surface: updating a display name, listing active sessions, and revoking one are all reachable today and are not reimplemented as Volt routes. Changing an email requires verification and is enabled through Better Auth rather than rebuilt.

Closing an account is the exception, because it is the one operation whose correctness depends on Volt's own invariants rather than on identity alone. Better Auth's `deleteUser` removes the user, their sessions, and their linked accounts; it knows nothing about memberships, and nothing about the rule that an organisation always has exactly one owner. A user who is the sole owner of an organisation therefore cannot close their account — the request is refused and they are told to transfer ownership or archive the organisation first — because the alternative is an organisation nobody can administer, holding settlement records nobody can reach.

Closure anonymises rather than erases. The user record keeps its identifier while its name and email are replaced with values that identify nobody and cannot be signed in with, sessions are revoked, and memberships are soft-deleted under the existing rules. Ledger events are left exactly as they are. They carry `actorUserId` and are hash-linked, so rewriting one to remove a reference would break the chain that makes the ledger tamper-evident, and the record of who accepted a settlement is the evidence the ledger exists to hold. What survives closure is an opaque identifier attached to energy and money records; what does not survive is any way to connect that identifier to a person. Preserving the audit trail while removing the identity is the trade being made, and it should be revisited if a jurisdiction demands erasure rather than anonymisation — at which point the honest answer is a documented exception process, not a silent rewrite of sealed events.
